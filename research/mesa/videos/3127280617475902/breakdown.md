## Why it may work, and where it is weak
Why it may work:
- It opens by establishing who is talking: a name plus Harvard Business School, on screen within half a second, held for about 1.5 seconds across three shots. For an Indian MBA audience that credential buys attention for the argument that follows.
- The verbal hook picks a fight with something every applicant knows and many dread, the CAT exam, and frames Mesa's process as closer to how real employers hire. That turns an admissions hurdle into a selling point.
- It lowers fear of the next step. In a retargeting campaign aimed at people who already showed interest, telling them concretely what the test involves (solve problems, sell an idea, explain to a 10-year-old) removes a reason to stall.
- The three test tasks are concrete and easy to picture, and "explain it to a 10-year-old" is memorable.
- The tone is of a senior person wishing the candidate well ("All the best"), which is softer than a hard sell and fits the admissions stage.

Where it is weak:
- After 2 seconds it is one man in one shot for 30 seconds with a single jump cut, no b-roll and no graphics. There is no visual of the test itself, which is the subject of the video.
- Captions are thin, small and unhighlighted. "CAT exam", "in-house" and the three tasks would be easy to miss with sound off.
- 188 words per minute is fast for a viewer reading captions, and the key name "MAT" is never spelled out or explained on screen in the sampled frames. Viewers may confuse it with the national MAT exam.
- His role at Mesa is never stated, so the "we" is not anchored. The card gives where he studied, not why he speaks for the school.
- The ad copy does not support the video: the headline is about AI agents and no-code and the text is about peers. Nothing in the copy mentions the test, CAT, or how to take the next step.
- No urgency or specifics: no test date, deadline, duration or link to book the test. The last 6.5 seconds are silent black and a static end card.
